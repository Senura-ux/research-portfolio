An Explainable Deep Learning Framework for
Multi-Disease Diagnosis and Disease-Specific
Assessment of Diabetes-Related Eye Disorders
Abstract—Diabetes mellitus is associated with ocular com-
plications such as Diabetic Retinopathy (DR), Diabetic Mac-
ular Edema (DME), cataract, and glaucoma, creating a need
for accessible and scalable diagnostic approaches. This paper
presents an Artificial Intelligence (AI)-driven framework for
the diagnosis of diabetes-related eye disorders. The framework
integrates four independently optimized disease-specific modules
within a unified web-based platform. Retinal fundus images are
analyzed for cataract, DR, and glaucoma, while Optical Coher-
ence Tomography (OCT) images are used for DME assessment.
The modules employ disease-specific deep learning approaches
and provide outputs including severity classification, risk as-
sessment, structural analysis, and explainable visualizations. The
cataract module additionally incorporates a visibility degradation
score derived from vessel visibility, sharpness, contrast, and
entropy. Experimental evaluation achieved validation accuracies
of 88.19%, 92.13%, 92.67%, and 95.48% for the cataract, DR,
DME, and glaucoma modules, respectively. These results demon-
strate the feasibility of integrating independently optimized mod-
els within a unified diagnostic workflow. By combining disease-
specific analysis, multimodal ophthalmic imaging, explainability,
and clinically oriented outputs, the proposed framework provides
an accessible, scalable, and interpretable approach to support the
diagnosis and assessment of diabetes-related eye disorders.
Index Terms—Deep Learning, Diabetes-Related Eye Disorders,
Multimodal Ophthalmic Imaging, Explainable Artificial Intelli-
gence, Automated Ophthalmic Diagnosis.
I. INTRODUCTION
Diabetes mellitus represents a major and increasing global
health burden, with its prevalence expected to continue rising
in the coming decades [1]. Beyond its systemic effects, dia-
betes is associated with several ocular complications that can
progressively impair vision, including Diabetic Retinopathy
(DR), Diabetic Macular Edema (DME), cataract, and glau-
coma. Vision impairment and blindness remain significant
global health concerns, highlighting the importance of early
detection and timely management of ocular disorders [2].
However, conventional ophthalmic screening relies heavily on
specialized clinical expertise and medical image interpretation,
which may limit the accessibility and scalability of large-scale
screening programs.
Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), particu-
larly Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL), have
created new opportunities for automated ophthalmic image
analysis. AI-based approaches have demonstrated the capa-
bility to identify disease-related patterns from medical im-
ages and support diagnostic decision-making processes [3].
Retinal fundus photography provides valuable information on
retinal and optic nerve structures for conditions such as DR,
glaucoma, and cataract, whereas Optical Coherence Tomogra-
phy (OCT) provides detailed cross-sectional information for
assessing retinal abnormalities associated with DME. Recent
developments in multimodal ophthalmic AI further highlight
the potential of combining imaging modalities with disease-
specific approaches [4].
Existing studies have demonstrated promising DL-based ap-
proaches for individual ophthalmic conditions. DL techniques
have been investigated for DR detection and severity classifi-
cation using fundus images [5], while explainable approaches
have been proposed for cataract grading to improve model
interpretability [6]. Similarly, Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs) have been applied to DME detection and classification
[7], and transfer learning has shown potential for automated
glaucoma detection from fundus images [8]. Recent research
has also explored multi-disease diagnosis using explainable
visualization techniques such as Gradient-weighted Class Ac-
tivation Mapping (Grad-CAM) [9].
Despite these advances, existing approaches often focus
on individual disease diagnosis or address multiple diseases
through frameworks that may not fully accommodate their
distinct pathological characteristics, imaging modalities, and
clinical assessment requirements. Since DR, DME, cataract,
and glaucoma require different diagnostic perspectives and
severity evaluation strategies, a standardized approach may not
provide optimal performance across all conditions. Therefore,
this study proposes an AI-driven multi-disease diagnostic
framework comprising four independently optimized disease-
specific modules integrated into a unified web-based platform.
The framework incorporates clinically oriented outputs, in-
cluding lesion-aware analysis for DR, retinal visibility degra-
dation assessment for cataract, risk-oriented outputs for DME
and glaucoma, and glaucoma-related structural assessment. By
integrating disease-specific AI models, multimodal ophthalmic
imaging, severity-oriented predictions, risk assessment, and
Grad-CAM-based visual explanations, the proposed frame-
work aims to provide an accessible, scalable, and clinically
interpretable solution for diabetes-related eye disorder diag-
nosis and assessment.
II. LITERATUREREVIEW
Diabetes is associated with several ocular complications,
including DR, DME, cataract, and glaucoma, which may
contribute to visual impairment [10]. The need for early

---PAGE 2---
and scalable screening has encouraged the adoption of AI,
particularly Deep Learning (DL), for automated ophthalmic
image analysis [3], [11]. Retinal fundus photography provides
information on retinal and optic nerve structures and has
been widely investigated for the detection of DR, glaucoma,
and cataract [10]. In contrast, OCT provides cross-sectional
structural information that is particularly valuable for assessing
retinal abnormalities associated with DME [3], [10]. Although
AI-based systems have demonstrated promising diagnostic
capabilities, many approaches focus on individual diseases or
limited groups of conditions [10], [11]. This highlights the
need for integrated frameworks that support multiple diabetes-
related ocular conditions while preserving disease-specific
diagnostic strategies.
Cataract assessment is relevant to diabetes-related ocular
screening because diabetes is associated with an increased risk
of cataract development and earlier cataract onset [12]. DL
techniques have been applied to automate cataract severity
grading from fundus images. Xu et al. proposed a hybrid
global-local CNN that combines global and fine-grained retinal
features, achieving 86.24% accuracy [13], while Zhou, Li,
and Li developed a discrete-state-transition neural network for
four-class grading [14]. Their findings also showed that retinal
structures, including blood vessels and the optic disc, become
progressively less visible as cataract severity increases [14].
Xu et al. further incorporated visual saliency, Grad-CAM, and
RNN-based semantic descriptions to improve interpretability
[6]. However, existing studies primarily emphasize severity
classification and explanation, with limited assessment of
cataract-related retinal visibility degradation and its impact on
fundus image interpretation.
DR is a major microvascular complication of diabetes that
progresses through increasing levels of retinal abnormality
[5]. CNN-based and transfer-learning approaches have demon-
strated strong potential for automated DR severity classifica-
tion. Recent lesion-based methods focus on clinically mean-
ingful features, including microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard
exudates, and cotton-wool spots [15]. Jiang et al. proposed
a ResNet-based multi-label model combined with Grad-CAM
to support lesion classification and localization [16]. Similarly,
Aouf et al. integrated transfer learning with Grad-CAM to pro-
vide interpretable visual evidence for retinal disease diagnosis
[9]. However, further research is needed to better integrate
lesion-level information, multi-class severity prediction, and
explainable outputs for clinical application.
Recent studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of
DL for automated DME assessment. Keerthana and Kumar
evaluated transfer-learning architectures, including Xception,
NASNetMobile, and ConvNeXt, for DME classification [7].
OCT-based analysis provides additional structural information
for retinal disease assessment. Elsharkawy et al. demon-
strated clinically explainable OCT analysis through abnormal-
ity detection and hierarchical grading based on meaningful
biomarkers [17]. However, existing studies often focus on
specific classification or grading tasks. Integrating OCT-based
recognition with severity or risk assessment and explainable
visual evidence remains relevant for more comprehensive
DME analysis within a diabetes-related eye disease diagnostic
framework.
AI-based fundus analysis has also been investigated for
glaucoma screening and structural assessment. Karim et al.
evaluated transfer-learned CNN models using cropped fundus
images focused on the optic disc and cup regions [8]. Au-
rangzeb et al. investigated automated glaucoma analysis using
retinal-vessel segmentation and optic cup/disc localization,
emphasizing structural biomarkers such as the cup-to-disc ratio
[11]. These studies demonstrate the value of combining image-
based learning with clinically relevant structural information.
However, many existing studies primarily emphasize classi-
fication or structural analysis, leaving scope for multi-stage
assessment, risk-oriented outputs, and explainable visual evi-
dence within a broader diabetes-related eye disease diagnostic
workflow.
Beyond diagnostic performance, transparency is increas-
ingly important for the clinical adoption of AI-based oph-
thalmic systems. Although DL models can achieve strong di-
agnostic performance, their black-box nature can limit clinical
trust and the interpretation of predictions [3]. Explainable AI
(XAI) techniques, such as Grad-CAM, address this limitation
by highlighting image regions that influence model predictions
[9]. Explainable approaches have been applied to cataract
assessment [6], DR analysis [9], and OCT-based retinal disease
grading [17]. However, clinically relevant features and imaging
characteristics differ across diseases, indicating the importance
of disease-specific visual explanations.
Despite advances in AI-based ophthalmic diagnosis, existing
approaches vary considerably in their imaging modalities,
diagnostic objectives, model architectures, and clinical outputs
[3], [10]. Multimodal systems also often emphasize data fusion
rather than maintaining disease-specific diagnostic strategies
suited to different conditions and imaging characteristics. To
address these limitations, this study proposes a modular AI-
driven framework for four diabetes-related ocular conditions,
namely cataract, DR, DME, and glaucoma. Each condition
is addressed using an independent disease-specific model and
appropriate imaging modality, while severity- or risk-related
outputs and explainable visualizations are provided through
a unified web-based platform. This approach combines a
consistent interface with diagnostic strategies tailored to the
characteristics of each disease.
III. METHODOLOGY
The proposed system is an explainable AI-driven framework
designed for the diagnosis of multiple diabetes-related eye dis-
orders through four independent diagnostic modules: cataract,
DR, DME, and glaucoma. As illustrated in Fig. 1, users select
the relevant disease module and upload the corresponding
fundus or OCT image. The selected module then performs
disease-specific analysis and generates the corresponding di-
agnostic output.

A. Cataract Severity and Retinal Structure Visibility Assess-
ment
1) Dataset and Preprocessing:The cataract module utilized
a public retinal fundus image dataset comprising 2,536 images
categorized as No, Mild, Moderate, and Severe cataract. The
dataset was divided into training (80%), validation (15%), and
held-out test (5%) sets. All images were resized to350×350
pixels. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization
(CLAHE) was applied to the luminance channel in the LAB
color space to enhance local contrast while preserving color
information. Training images were augmented to improve
model generalization, followed by ImageNet-based normaliza-
tion before model training.
2) Cataract Classification Model:Cataract severity was
classified using a fine-tuned EfficientNet-B3 model based on
transfer learning, with the original classifier replaced by a
custom four-class classification head. The model was trained
for 70 epochs using Focal Loss to improve learning from
difficult samples. Mixup and CutMix were incorporated as
regularization strategies to enhance robustness and reduce
overfitting, while AdamW optimization and Exponential Mov-
ing Average (EMA) were employed to support stable training
and improved generalization.
3) Visibility Degradation Assessment and Explainability:
To complement CNN-based cataract severity prediction, retinal
visibility degradation was assessed using four handcrafted
image-quality measures related to vessel visibility, sharpness,
contrast, and entropy, extracted from CLAHE-preprocessed
fundus images. Vessel degradation was estimated using multi-
scale Difference of Gaussians responses, sharpness degra-
dation using Laplacian variance, contrast degradation using
percentile-based Michelson contrast, and information degra-
dation using local entropy. Ridge regression with standardized
predictors and 5-fold cross-validation was employed to learn
their relative contributions.
The handcrafted visibility score was calculated as
V S=w V V+w ShSh+w CC+w EE,(1)
whereV,Sh,C, andEdenote vessel, sharpness, contrast,
and entropy degradation scores, respectively, andw V ,w Sh,
wC, andw E are normalized learned weights.
The CNN output probabilities were converted into a nor-
malized severity score. The final visibility degradation score
was computed as
V DS=αS+βV S,(2)
whereSis the CNN-derived severity score,V Sis the hand-
crafted visibility score, andαandβare fusion coefficients.
Grad-CAM was used to highlight image regions contribut-
ing to the CNN prediction.
B. DR Severity Classification
1) Dataset and Preprocessing:The DR classification
dataset consisted of retinal fundus images representing five
severity classes: No DR, Mild, Moderate, Severe, and Prolif-
erative DR. To address class imbalance, 1,000 original images
were selected from each class using a fixed random seed. An
additional 2,000 augmented images per class were generated,
resulting in a balanced dataset of 15,000 images. Augmenta-
tion included horizontal and vertical flipping, random rotation
within±15 ◦, brightness, contrast, and saturation adjustment,
Gaussian blur, and Gaussian noise. Images were resized to
384×384pixels, converted to tensors, and normalized. The
dataset was divided using an 85:15 training and validation
split.
2) DR Classification Model:The DR severity classification
model was developed using EfficientNetV2-S with ImageNet
pretrained weights and transfer learning. Initially, the pre-
trained backbone was frozen and only a custom classification

head containing fully connected layers, SiLU activation, batch
normalization, and dropout was trained. The last four back-
bone blocks were subsequently unfrozen for fine-tuning. Dif-
ferent learning rates were applied to the pretrained backbone
and classification head. AdamW with CosineAnnealingWarm-
Restarts was used for optimization, while OrdinalSmoothLoss,
label smoothing, mixed-precision training, gradient clipping,
and early stopping were applied to improve training stability
and generalization.
3) Lesion-Aware Explainability:Grad-CAM was integrated
after classification to highlight retinal regions contributing to
the predicted DR severity. The activation map was combined
with the original fundus image to provide an interpretable
visualization and support examination of lesion-related retinal
regions.
C. DME Detection and Classification
1) Dataset and OCT Image Processing:The proposed
DME module uses the publicly available Retinal OCT Images
dataset from Kaggle, containing 84,495 OCT images classified
into CNV , DME, DRUSEN, and NORMAL categories. Images
are resized to224×224pixels and normalized to provide con-
sistent model inputs. Data augmentation, including rotation,
horizontal flipping, and brightness adjustment, is applied to
increase data diversity and improve model generalization.
2) DME Classification Model:An EfficientNet-B0 model
is employed for OCT image classification due to its balance
between predictive performance and computational efficiency.
Transfer learning is applied using pretrained weights, followed
by fine-tuning on the OCT dataset. The final classification
layer is modified to distinguish the four retinal conditions:
NORMAL, DRUSEN, DME, and CNV . The model learns
disease-related retinal patterns, including fluid accumulation
and structural abnormalities.
3) Risk Assessment and Explainability:Classification prob-
abilities are further utilized to provide risk-aware diagnostic
information. Calibrated thresholds assign Low, Medium, or
High risk levels based on prediction confidence. Grad-CAM
generates heatmaps highlighting retinal regions that influence
predictions, improving model interpretability. The final output
presents the predicted condition, confidence score, risk level,
and corresponding Grad-CAM visualization.
D. Glaucoma Detection and Risk Assessment
1) Dataset and Image Preprocessing:A total of 1,540 reti-
nal fundus images representing Normal, Early Glaucoma, and
Advanced Glaucoma were used to develop the classification
module. The images were divided into training, validation,
and test sets, resized to224×224pixels, and normalized
using ImageNet statistics. Data augmentation, including flip-
ping, rotation, affine transformations, color adjustments, and
random erasing, was applied to improve generalization. Class
imbalance was addressed using weighted random sampling.
2) Glaucoma Classification Model:Transfer learning was
implemented using EfficientNet-B0 with ImageNet-pretrained
weights. The original classification head was replaced with a
three-class classifier. Training was performed in two stages by
initially freezing the feature-extraction backbone and subse-
quently fine-tuning the entire network. AdamW optimization,
learning-rate scheduling, class-weighted loss, label smoothing,
MixUp, gradient clipping, and early stopping were used to im-
prove training stability and reduce overfitting. The final model
generated class probabilities, with the highest-probability class
selected as the predicted glaucoma stage.
3) Risk Prediction and Explainability:The predicted prob-
abilities were used to report the glaucoma stage and confidence
score, supporting severity-oriented assessment rather than bi-
nary classification. Test-time augmentation was applied during
inference to improve prediction robustness. Performance was
evaluated using precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrices,
and one-versus-rest ROC-AUC analysis. Grad-CAM was used
to visualize influential image regions, while out-of-distribution
checking and cup-to-disc ratio analysis provided additional
information for interpreting the model output.
Fig. 3 illustrates a representative Grad-CAM visualization
used to interpret the model’s image-based predictions.
Fig. 3. Representative Grad-CAM visualization for model interpretation: (a)

E. Out-of-Distribution Detection
To assess input reliability, each disease-specific module in-
corporates Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection. Deep feature
embeddings are modeled using class-wise means and a shared
covariance matrix, while the minimum Mahalanobis distance
is used as the OOD score. A module-specific threshold is
calibrated at the relevant validation-set percentile to identify
inputs that differ substantially from the corresponding training
distribution.
F . Web-Based System Integration and Deployment
The system was deployed as a web-based platform with a
React frontend and independent Flask-based services for each
disease module. Disease-specific pages allow users to upload
the relevant images and send them to the corresponding back-
end services for model inference. The resulting predictions,
confidence scores, and visual explanations are then displayed
within the respective disease interfaces.

IV. RESULTS ANDDISCUSSION
The proposed framework was evaluated through four in-
dependently developed disease-specific modules for cataract,
DR, DME, and glaucoma. Classification performance was
assessed using validation accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-
score, together with qualitative evaluation of disease-specific
outputs and explainability components.
The EfficientNet-B3 cataract classifier achieved 88.19% val-
idation accuracy, with macro-average precision, recall, and F1-
scores of 88.51%, 88.40%, and 88.39%, respectively. Severe
cataract achieved the highest recall of 96.55%, with strong
class discrimination shown in Fig. 4. Beyond classification, the
proposed VDS combined CNN-derived severity with retinal
image-quality measures. Ridge regression identified entropy
(0.4426), contrast (0.2955), vessel visibility (0.2287), and
sharpness (0.0332) as contributing factors for complementary
retinal visibility assessment.
Fig. 4. Confusion matrix of the EfficientNet-B3-based cataract severity
classification model.
The EfficientNetV2-S DR module achieved an overall val-
idation accuracy of 92.13%, with weighted precision, recall,
and F1-scores of 91.70%, 92.13%, and 92.14%, respectively.
Proliferative DR achieved the highest F1-score of 94.74%,
whereas Mild DR achieved 89.79%. As illustrated in Fig. 5,
most predictions were correctly classified, although some
confusion occurred between neighbouring severity stages.
Fig. 5. Confusion matrix of the EfficientNetV2-S-based diabetic retinopathy
severity classification model.
The EfficientNet-B0 DME module achieved a best valida-
tion accuracy of 92.67%, with precision, recall, and F1-scores
of 92.77%, 92.67%, and 92.64%, respectively. The model
successfully classified the four OCT categories: CNV , DME,
DRUSEN, and NORMAL, with DME achieving the highest
class-wise F1-score of 95.63%. Fig. 6 shows strong diagonal
classification performance across the four categories. Predic-
tion probabilities and Grad-CAM outputs further supported
DME risk assessment and prediction interpretation.
Fig. 6. Confusion matrix of the EfficientNet-B0-based diabetic macular edema
classification model.
The glaucoma module, based on EfficientNet-B0 transfer
learning, achieved a best validation accuracy of 95.48% and
demonstrated strong discrimination among Normal, Early, and
Advanced Glaucoma, as shown in Fig. 7. Class-wise F1-scores
ranged from 94.70% to 95.98%, while AUC values exceeded
0.99 for all classes. Grad-CAM, out-of-distribution checking,
and cup-to-disc ratio analysis further supported prediction
interpretability and reliability.
Fig. 7. Confusion matrix of the EfficientNet-B0-based glaucoma classification
model.

V. CONCLUSION
This study presented an AI-driven multi-disease diagnostic
framework for assessing four diabetes-related eye disorders:
cataract, DR, DME, and glaucoma. The framework integrates
disease-specific deep learning models with complementary
outputs, including severity grading, risk assessment, prediction
confidence, and explainable visual analysis, to support a more
interpretable diagnostic workflow.
Experimental results demonstrated strong performance
across the individual disease-specific modules. The cataract
module achieved a validation accuracy of 88.19% while ad-
ditionally providing a visibility degradation score based on
retinal image-quality measures. The DR, DME, and glau-
coma modules achieved validation accuracies of 92.13%,
92.67%, and 95.48% respectively, supporting disease classi-
fication together with severity- and risk-oriented assessment.
The glaucoma module also demonstrated strong class-wise
performance, particularly in identifying Early and Advanced
Glaucoma.
Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates the feasi-
bility of integrating multiple AI-based diagnostic functions
into a unified and explainable workflow. Future work will
focus on validating the framework using diverse external
clinical datasets, further integrating the individual disease-
specific modules, and evaluating the proposed decision-support
workflow in clinical settings to improve its robustness, gener-
alizability, and practical clinical usefulness.
